Car Preparedness for Winter Driving, Not Just Mild Frost

Equipping your car for winter means more than just clearing the windscreen. Be ready for breakdowns and severe conditions, not just a light dusting.

The fashionhotspot editors10 picks

Winter driving introduces a new set of challenges beyond typical commutes. Most drivers will have an ice scraper, perhaps some de-icer, and feel prepared for a light frost. This approach often overlooks the more serious risks: sudden breakdowns in freezing temperatures, getting stuck in deep snow, or being stranded on an unplowed road for an extended period. Relying solely on basic items can leave you vulnerable when conditions worsen, transforming a minor inconvenience into a genuine hazard. Preparation is about anticipating the worst, not just hoping for the best.

The market offers a wide array of winter car accessories, many of which are more novelty than necessity. Deciding what genuinely merits space in your vehicle requires a practical, sometimes sceptical, approach. Focus on items that address critical safety concerns: maintaining visibility, ensuring vehicle mobility, and providing personal warmth and communication in an emergency. This guide cuts through the noise, recommending gear chosen for its fundamental utility and reliability when you most need it, rather than for its marketing claims or convenience features.

04 · Check tyre pressure precisely

JACO ElitePro Digital Tire Pressure Gauge

Tire pressure drops significantly in cold weather – about one PSI for every 10-degree Fahrenheit drop. Under-inflated tires reduce fuel economy, increase wear, and, critically, impair handling and traction, which is dangerous on icy or snowy roads. A digital tire pressure gauge provides accurate readings, which is important because the difference between optimal pressure and under-inflation can be subtle but impactful. Checking your tires when they are cold, before driving, gives the most accurate reading. While manual gauges exist, digital models are often easier to read quickly in poor light or cold conditions, despite relying on batteries.

For minor traction issues on ice or compacted snow, a bag of non-clumping kitty litter or sand can be remarkably effective. Common questions

Do I need snow tires?

Snow tires offer superior traction and stopping in snow and ice compared to all-season tires. Their specialized rubber compounds remain flexible in low temperatures, and aggressive tread patterns bite into snow. However, they are a significant investment, requiring a second set of wheels or tire changes twice a year. For most drivers in areas with infrequent heavy snow, good all-season tires and careful driving are often sufficient. They are most critical for those in truly severe winter climates or with hilly commutes, where the performance difference is pronounced. Evaluate your specific driving environment before committing.

Is it safe to leave my car running to warm up?

Idling a modern car for extended periods to warm it up is generally unnecessary and wastes fuel. Modern engines are designed to be driven shortly after starting, allowing them to reach optimal operating temperature more quickly and efficiently. Prolonged idling can also increase emissions and potentially lead to carbon buildup. In extreme cold, running the car for a few minutes to circulate fluids is reasonable, but sitting for 15-20 minutes is not beneficial for the engine or your wallet. Focus on clearing windows and ensuring visibility before driving, rather than extended idling.

What about an emergency roadside assistance membership?

Roadside assistance programs, often through your insurance, car manufacturer, or independent clubs, can be valuable. They provide services like towing, jump-starts, tire changes, and fuel delivery. For many, the peace of mind is worth the annual fee, especially if you drive frequently or travel long distances. However, these services can sometimes have long wait times during peak demand, like after a major snowstorm. They are not a substitute for carrying essential gear yourself, as you might still need to manage a situation before help arrives. Assess your typical driving conditions and personal comfort with self-reliance before committing.

Should I carry extra fuel in my car?

Carrying extra fuel in your car is generally not recommended for safety reasons. Fuel containers, even approved ones, can leak or be damaged in a collision, posing a significant fire hazard. The fumes can also be unpleasant and potentially harmful if not properly ventilated. If you are concerned about running out of fuel, the better approach is to simply keep your tank at least half full during winter months, especially before longer trips or when severe weather is forecast. This also provides extra weight over the drive wheels for some vehicles, slightly improving traction.
