The Smart Home Parts That Are Actually Worth Wiring In

Most smart home gear solves a problem you did not have. These ten solve real ones — and we say plainly which ones we would skip.

The fashionhotspot editors10 picks

The smart home has a credibility problem, and it earned it. For a decade the pitch was that you would talk to your lights, and the reality was an app for every socket, a hub that stopped being supported, and a light switch your guests could not work out.

What changed is Matter and Thread — a shared standard that finally lets devices from different brands talk to each other without a pile of bridges. It is not perfect, but it means a purchase made today has a reasonable chance of still working in five years, which was not true before.

So the test here is simple: does it save real time or real money, and will it survive its manufacturer losing interest. Where a category fails that test, we say so rather than filling the slot.

The picks

01 · The one that pays for itself

Ecobee or Nest Learning Thermostat

Heating and cooling is the largest energy line in most homes, which makes the thermostat the only smart device with a credible payback argument. The saving does not come from talking to it — it comes from the schedule and from remote sensors that stop the system heating an empty room while the occupied one stays cold. Ecobee ships those sensors in the box, which is the practical advantage over Nest. Two honest limits: if your home is small and single-zone the saving is modest, and if you rent, check whether you are allowed to swap the unit at all.

02 · Best lighting, at a price

Philips Hue starter kit (bridge + bulbs)

Hue is the expensive option and it is still the one to buy, because lighting is where reliability matters most — a bulb that takes two seconds to respond, or fails once a week, is worse than a dumb switch. The bridge is what buys that reliability: it runs the lights locally over Zigbee rather than routing every command through the internet. Where the real value shows is scheduling, not colour. Lights that shift warm in the evening and come up gradually in the morning change how a room feels far more than any party scene ever will.

03 · Cheapest thing that makes the rest useful

Aqara or Eve door and window sensors

Automations are only as good as what the house knows about itself, and contact sensors are the cheapest way to give it useful facts. A door sensor is what turns the hall light on when you come in, tells you the back door has been open for twenty minutes in winter, and warns you the garage never closed. None of that requires a camera or a subscription. Buy Thread or Zigbee versions rather than Wi-Fi ones — Wi-Fi sensors chew batteries and crowd your network, and you may end up with a dozen of these.

04 · Biggest daily convenience

Aqara U100 or Yale Assure Lock 2

The smart lock is the device people are most sceptical about and end up using most, because it removes a small friction that occurs several times a day. Handing a cleaner or a guest a temporary code instead of cutting a key is the feature that converts people. Insist on two things: a physical key override, and standard AA batteries rather than a proprietary pack. Check your door before buying — these fit standard deadbolts, and European multipoint locks or unusual Israeli door hardware often need a different model entirely, so measure first.

05 · Best time saved per shekel

Roborock or Dreame robot vacuum with self-empty

This is the appliance that buys back the most time, provided you buy the right tier. The two features that matter are lidar mapping, so it cleans in rows rather than bouncing randomly, and a self-emptying dock, so you handle dust every few weeks rather than every day. Below roughly $400 you generally lose one or both and the novelty wears off fast. Be realistic about what it replaces: it handles daily maintenance beautifully and does not replace a proper clean. It also needs a tidy floor, so it quietly enforces a habit — which some people count as a feature.

06 · Best starting point

Eve Energy or TP-Link Tapo smart plugs

A smart plug is the cheapest way to find out whether you actually want a smart home. It makes a dumb lamp, fan or heater schedulable, and the models with energy monitoring answer a question most people are guessing at — which appliance is quietly costing the most. That measurement is often more valuable than the automation. Get Matter-compatible plugs so they work with whatever assistant you end up using, and avoid unbranded ones: this is a device that carries mains current and lives in your wall for years.

07 · Best insurance for the money

Aqara water leak sensor

A twenty dollar puck under the washing machine, the boiler and the sink is the highest return-per-shekel item on this page, and nobody buys it until after their first flood. Water damage is slow, expensive and usually discovered far too late — behind an appliance, into a floor, through a ceiling. These sit flat, run for a year or two on a coin cell, and scream and notify your phone the moment they get wet. There is no ongoing cost and nothing to maintain. If you buy one thing from this list, and you have never had a leak, buy this one anyway.

08 · Best hub, with reservations

Echo Show 8 or Nest Hub

A smart display earns its place in a kitchen, where hands are busy and a timer, a recipe and a camera feed on one screen genuinely help. Many of these units also act as a Thread border router or Zigbee hub, which quietly saves you buying a separate bridge. The reservation is real, though: this is a microphone and a camera in a room you live in, sold at a low margin because the business model is elsewhere. If that trade bothers you, cover the camera, mute the mic when not in use, or buy a display-free hub instead — that is a legitimate choice, not paranoia.

09 · The upgrade over motion sensors

Aqara FP2 presence sensor

The reason smart lighting annoys people is that motion sensors detect motion, not presence — so the lights go out while you are reading, sitting still, or in the bath. Millimetre-wave presence sensors detect breathing and micro-movement, so they know the room is occupied even when nothing is moving. That single distinction is what makes automated lighting stop being irritating. It is more expensive than a motion sensor and needs mains power and a careful mounting position, so treat it as a fix for the one or two rooms where the lights keep failing you, not as something to install everywhere.

10 · Best camera without a subscription

Eufy or Reolink local-storage camera

Most security cameras are sold cheap and monetised through a monthly plan, and if you stop paying you often lose recording entirely. Cameras that record to a local card or a base station avoid that, so you own the footage and there is no recurring bill. Look for local recording, on-device person detection so you are not alerted by every passing cat, and RTSP support if you ever want to run your own recorder. Point them at entrances and outdoor spaces rather than inside living areas, and check the rules where you live — recording shared entrances or a neighbour's property has legal limits in most places.

Common questions

What is Matter, and do I need to care?

It is a shared standard that lets devices from different brands work together and be controlled by any major assistant. You should care mainly because it protects you from buying into a brand that later shuts its servers down.

What smart home gear would you skip?

Smart fridges, smart microwaves and most smart taps — the intelligence adds cost and failure modes to an appliance you keep for fifteen years. Also smart blinds unless you have a specific sun or accessibility problem, given the installation cost.

Will any of this still work if my internet goes down?

Devices on Zigbee, Thread or a local hub mostly keep working, including schedules. Anything that depends on a manufacturer's cloud generally stops. That is the single best reason to prefer local control when you have the choice.

I rent. What can I install?

Plugs, bulbs, sensors and a robot vacuum all install without changing anything permanent. Thermostats and locks usually need permission, though some locks replace only the interior side and leave the existing cylinder and key untouched.
